💰 Public Finance – 2: The Story of Government Money

Simple, story-based notes covering the concepts, formulas, laws, committees and UPSC questions in your 51-page PDF.

Imagine India's government is running a huge household. It collects taxes, pays salaries, builds roads, provides food subsidies, borrows money and repays old loans. Sometimes its expenses exceed its income. To understand how the government manages this situation, we need to follow the journey of its money.

Contents

    🏦Chapter 1: The government has three money boxes

    Imagine the government has three different boxes. Each box has a different purpose, and you cannot treat them as the same thing.

    1Consolidated Fund of India (CFI)

    Article 266(1)

    This is the government's main money box. Income from taxes, non-tax revenue, loans raised and repayments received are credited here.

    The government pays for most of its regular activities and development expenditure from this fund. Spending generally requires Parliament's authorisation through the Appropriation Act.

    2Public Account of India (PA)

    Article 266(2)

    Imagine your friend gives you ₹1,000 to keep safely. You are holding the money, but it is not your income. You are acting as a trustee.

    Similarly, the government holds money that belongs to depositors or must be accounted for on their behalf.

    Examples include provident funds, small savings, postal deposits, the National Small Savings Fund and other specified funds.

    Parliament's prior approval is generally not required for withdrawals, but the money must still be properly accounted for.

    3Contingency Fund of India

    Article 267(1)

    Imagine a sudden flood destroys a bridge. The government needs money immediately and cannot wait for the usual spending process.

    This fund helps meet unforeseen or emergency expenditure. It is at the disposal of the President of India and is operated through the Finance Ministry's prescribed arrangements.

    Once money is used, Parliament's approval is obtained for the necessary expenditure and the fund is replenished.

    The easiest way to remember

    Fund Imagine it as Parliament's role
    Consolidated Fund Main government account Prior authorisation required for expenditure
    Public Account Money held in trust Prior approval for withdrawal generally not required
    Contingency Fund Emergency cash reserve Later approval and replenishment
    UPSC trap All government money does not follow the same withdrawal rules. In the Public Account, the government often acts as a trustee rather than as the owner of the deposited money.

    ⚖️Chapter 2: The government spends more than it earns

    Suppose a government collects ₹100, but its total expenditure is ₹130. It has a shortfall of ₹30.

    That shortfall is called a deficit.

    Deficit=Expenditure − Income

    But the government has different types of income and expenditure. Therefore, economists calculate different kinds of deficits to discover exactly where the problem lies.

    Story 1: Revenue deficit — the household cannot cover daily expenses

    Imagine a family earns ₹60,000 per month but spends ₹80,000 on food, electricity, salaries of household staff and other regular needs.

    It is short by ₹20,000. It is borrowing even to meet its daily expenses.

    The government faces a similar situation when its regular revenue cannot cover its regular expenditure.

    Revenue Deficit=Revenue Expenditure − Revenue Receipts

    Example:

    • Revenue expenditure = ₹80,000 crore
    • Revenue receipts = ₹60,000 crore
    • Revenue deficit = ₹20,000 crore

    This indicates that the government is not earning enough revenue to meet its routine expenditure. A persistent revenue deficit can be fiscally unsustainable.

    Fiscal prudence Reducing the revenue deficit means improving the government's ability to meet current expenses from its revenue.

    Story 2: Effective revenue deficit — some spending actually creates assets

    Now imagine that the government gives ₹5 lakh crore in revenue grants to states. Out of this, ₹1 lakh crore is specifically used by the states to build capital assets such as roads, schools and hospitals.

    Although these grants are recorded as revenue expenditure in the Union Government's accounts, they help create assets.

    Should all ₹5 lakh crore be treated as unproductive routine spending? Not quite.

    This is why the government introduced the Effective Revenue Deficit (ERD) in the Union Budget 2011–12.

    ERD=Revenue Deficit − Grants for Capital Asset Creation

    Example:

    • Revenue deficit = ₹5 lakh crore
    • Grants for creating capital assets = ₹1 lakh crore
    • Effective revenue deficit = ₹4 lakh crore

    ERD gives a clearer picture of the portion of revenue deficit that is not accounted for by these asset-creating grants.

    Story 3: Fiscal deficit — how much money must the government borrow?

    Return to our household. Suppose its total expenditure is ₹130, but its non-borrowed receipts are only ₹100. It must arrange the remaining ₹30, usually through borrowing.

    This is the central idea behind the fiscal deficit.

    Fiscal Deficit=Total Expenditure − (Revenue Receipts +Non-debt Capital Receipts)

    Non-debt capital receipts include recoveries of loans and disinvestment proceeds, among other items.

    Why exclude borrowings from receipts? Because borrowing is how the government finances its gap. If we count the borrowed money as income, the gap disappears on paper even though the government has taken on a new liability.

    Remember Fiscal deficit shows the government's overall borrowing requirement before financing that gap.

    Story 4: Primary deficit — forget the burden of old loans for a moment

    Imagine the government has a fiscal deficit of ₹50,000 crore. Out of its expenses, ₹15,000 crore goes towards paying interest on loans taken in previous years.

    How much is the deficit after excluding this old interest burden?

    Primary Deficit=Fiscal Deficit − Interest Payments

    Here:

    • Fiscal deficit = ₹50,000 crore
    • Interest payments = ₹15,000 crore
    • Primary deficit = ₹35,000 crore

    The primary deficit tells us how much of the current fiscal gap remains after excluding interest payments on past debt.

    If the primary deficit is zero, the fiscal deficit is equal to interest payments. This does not mean the government has no deficit; it means the entire deficit corresponds to interest payments.

    Story 5: Monetised deficit — the central bank helps finance the gap

    Suppose the government needs to borrow money, and the Reserve Bank of India (RBI) directly provides financing by creating new money or purchasing government securities directly from the government.

    This is the idea behind a monetised deficit.

    Monetised Deficit=Net RBI Credit to Government

    The important concern is inflation. If money supply expands excessively relative to the goods and services available, prices may rise.

    However, monetary financing may be considered in exceptional circumstances. Your notes explain that routine monetised deficit financing is restricted under the FRBM framework, while exceptional situations may be treated differently.

    Story 6: Budget deficit — the simplest calculation

    The broadest calculation in your notes is:

    Budget Deficit=Total Expenditure − Total Receipts

    Unlike fiscal deficit, this basic formula includes total receipts as presented, including borrowings. Fiscal deficit excludes borrowings from the receipt side.

    Here is the complete family of deficit indicators:

    Type Formula What it tells us
    Budget deficit Total expenditure − total receipts Overall shortfall under the stated definition
    Revenue deficit Revenue expenditure − revenue receipts Gap in routine government finances
    Effective revenue deficit Revenue deficit − capital-asset grants Revenue deficit after adjusting for asset-creating grants
    Fiscal deficit Total expenditure − non-borrowed receipts Overall borrowing requirement
    Primary deficit Fiscal deficit − interest payments Fiscal gap excluding past interest burden
    Monetised deficit Net RBI credit to government Deficit financed through RBI credit

    The crucial difference: receipts and income

    Your notes make an important distinction.

    • Receipts: Money coming into the government, including borrowings.
    • Non-borrowed receipts used in fiscal-deficit calculations: Revenue receipts plus non-debt capital receipts.

    Borrowing brings cash into the government's account, but it also creates a repayment obligation. That is why fiscal deficit excludes borrowing from its receipt calculation.

    📈Chapter 3: Public debt — the loans the government must carry

    Imagine the government has borrowed money over several years to build highways, pay expenses and finance deficits. Even if one year's deficit falls, the earlier loans do not automatically disappear.

    Public debt is the outstanding borrowing of the government at a particular time.

    Your notes divide it into two main categories.

    1Internal debt

    The government borrows within India, generally in Indian rupees.

    Examples listed in your notes include Treasury Bills, dated government securities, bonds held by LIC, banks and the public, and specified RBI facilities such as Ways and Means Advances (WMA).

    2External debt

    The government borrows from foreign governments or international institutions such as the IMF, World Bank and Asian Development Bank.

    External borrowing may involve foreign currencies, creating exposure to exchange-rate fluctuations. Repayment may therefore require foreign exchange.

    Public debt versus other liabilities

    Your notes also distinguish between:

    • Public debt: Borrowings with a repayment obligation, such as loans and bonds.
    • Other liabilities: Obligations such as small savings and provident fund balances, which are not necessarily classified as conventional public debt but still have to be accounted for.

    The expression public debt and other liabilities includes outstanding liabilities arising from various small-savings schemes, provident funds and similar arrangements.

    One important qualification: the notes' simplified description of external debt should not be read as meaning that every foreign-related borrowing is sovereign debt. Private external borrowings and NRI deposits are not automatically sovereign public debt.

    Who manages public debt?

    Your notes identify the Reserve Bank of India as an important public-debt manager, referring to the Public Debt Act, 1944.

    What happens when public debt becomes too high?

    Imagine that the family spends an increasing part of its monthly income just paying interest on old loans. It then has less money for education, healthcare and other needs.

    The government faces a similar problem:

    1. Higher interest burden: More revenue goes towards interest payments.
    2. Crowding out: Government borrowing may absorb funds that could otherwise support private investment.
    3. Possible credit-rating downgrades: Investors may become more concerned about the government's ability to repay.
    4. Reduced fiscal space: Less money remains available for future development or emergencies.

    Story 7: Off-budget borrowing — a loan hidden behind another name

    Suppose a government wants to provide food subsidies. Instead of borrowing directly through the regular budget, a public sector undertaking or special-purpose vehicle takes a loan to finance the activity.

    The expenditure may support a government programme, but the borrowing may not appear as part of the officially reported fiscal deficit in the same way as direct budget borrowing.

    This is called off-budget borrowing.

    Your notes mention:

    • Loans raised through public sector undertakings (PSUs) or special-purpose vehicles (SPVs).
    • Government guarantees for loans.
    • Bonds issued by institutions such as the Food Corporation of India (FCI) and Power Finance Corporation (PFC).
    • An example in which FCI borrows from the National Small Savings Fund to finance food subsidy requirements rather than receiving the entire amount through a direct budget allocation.

    Why is this a concern? The liability still exists even if it is not immediately visible in the headline fiscal-deficit figure. It can reduce transparency and make the government's true financial burden harder to assess.

    📜Chapter 4: The FRBM Act — the government promises to control its spending

    Imagine a household that repeatedly borrows without a repayment plan. Eventually, its interest burden grows and lenders become worried.

    India faced concerns about high fiscal deficits, rising public debt and weak fiscal discipline. Following the economic reforms of 1991, the need for a more rules-based fiscal framework became increasingly important.

    This led to the Fiscal Responsibility and Budget Management (FRBM) Act, 2003.

    Its central idea is simple: the government should manage its finances responsibly, reduce deficits and debt, disclose its fiscal plans and support macroeconomic stability.

    What did the original 2003 Act provide?

    Your notes list these original targets:

    • Revenue deficit: reduce it to zero by 2008.
    • Fiscal deficit: reduce it to 3% of GDP by 2009.

    GDP means the total value of final goods and services produced in an economy during a given period. Expressing a deficit as a percentage of GDP allows us to compare it with the size of the economy.

    The Act also required the government to present three important documents annually:

    1. Medium-Term Fiscal Policy Statement (MTFPS): A medium-term picture of fiscal indicators and targets.
    2. Fiscal Policy Strategy Statement (FPSS): The government's fiscal priorities and strategy.
    3. Macroeconomic Framework Statement (MFS): An overview of the economy and its key macroeconomic indicators.

    The original framework also restricted direct RBI financing of the fiscal deficit through primary issuances of government securities.

    Why did the original rules face criticism?

    Imagine telling a household that it must reduce borrowing every year, regardless of whether someone loses their job or faces a medical emergency.

    A rigid rule can become difficult to follow when circumstances change. The FRBM framework faced similar criticisms:

    • Targets were repeatedly missed or postponed, including after the global financial crisis.
    • Rules could be too rigid during economic downturns.
    • The framework did not sufficiently distinguish between borrowing for consumption and borrowing for productive investment.
    • It did not adequately account for cyclical changes in the economy.

    This raised a difficult question: should the government always reduce its deficit immediately, or should it have some flexibility during a crisis?

    Story 8: The 2012 amendment — flexibility and a better measure

    The FRBM Amendment of 2012 revised the fiscal framework and allowed more realistic timeframes.

    According to your notes, it provided for:

    • Fiscal deficit of 3% of GDP by 2017.
    • Revenue deficit below 2% of GDP by 2015.
    • Effective revenue deficit of zero by 2015.

    It also introduced the Medium-Term Expenditure Framework, which sets out a rolling three-year framework for expenditure indicators.

    The key development was the formal introduction of ERD into the fiscal framework. It recognised that some grants recorded as revenue expenditure help create capital assets.

    These dates are the historical targets in your PDF, not a statement that each target was actually achieved.

    Story 9: The N. K. Singh Committee — should debt be the main anchor?

    In 2017, the government established the FRBM Review Committee, chaired by N. K. Singh, to review the existing framework and recommend a more modern fiscal system.

    Instead of focusing only on annual deficit targets, the committee proposed a broader approach based on the government's total debt.

    Its main recommendations, as given in your notes, were:

    Recommendation Simple meaning
    Debt-to-GDP anchor Aim for combined government debt of 60% of GDP: 40% for the Centre and 20% for the states
    Fiscal-deficit glide path Gradually reduce the fiscal deficit, with 2.5% of GDP suggested for 2023, subject to later revisions
    Remove the revenue-deficit target Assess overall fiscal health rather than focusing only on the revenue side
    Escape clause Allow deviations of up to 0.5% of GDP in specified situations such as national emergencies, war and natural disasters
    Fiscal Council Establish an independent body to oversee and assess fiscal policy
    Why does this matter?

    Suppose two governments each have a fiscal deficit of ₹1 lakh crore. One may have a relatively small accumulated debt, while the other may already carry a very large debt burden.

    Their financial situations are not identical. Looking at the debt-to-GDP ratio helps assess the accumulated burden relative to the economy's capacity.

    A fiscal deficit measures a gap during a period; public debt measures accumulated liabilities at a point in time.

    What did your PDF say about the position in 2025–26?

    The presentation gives the following historical snapshot:

    • The COVID-19 crisis led to a sharp deviation from the earlier fiscal-consolidation path.
    • The revised estimate for the Centre's fiscal deficit in 2024–25 was 4.8% of GDP.
    • The Budget Estimate for 2025–26 was 4.4% of GDP.
    • The 3% fiscal-deficit target was described as deferred to 2025–26 or beyond.
    • The proposed independent Fiscal Council had not yet been established, according to the presentation.

    These are the figures and status reported by your notes. They should not be treated as the latest fiscal position for October 2026.

    🛠️Chapter 5: Fiscal consolidation — repairing the government's finances

    Imagine that the government wants to reduce its borrowing without damaging development. It cannot simply stop spending on everything. It must increase revenue, reduce waste and spend more efficiently.

    This process is called fiscal consolidation.

    Its aims are to reduce deficits and debt sustainably, maintain macroeconomic stability, reduce the interest burden and preserve resources for future generations.

    Your notes identify seven approaches:

    1Increase revenue

    Expand the tax base, improve tax compliance through systems such as GST, and raise non-tax revenue through dividends, disinvestment and spectrum auctions.

    2Rationalise expenditure

    Target subsidies better through mechanisms such as Direct Benefit Transfer (DBT), reduce wasteful spending and control the growth of revenue expenditure.

    3Disinvestment and monetisation

    Sell government stakes in public sector enterprises where appropriate and generate revenue from existing public assets.

    4Control the fiscal deficit

    Follow a credible fiscal path and use medium-term fiscal policy statements to guide decisions.

    5Manage debt efficiently

    Prefer suitable, lower-cost, long-term borrowing and improve transparency by avoiding hidden off-budget liabilities.

    6Reform subsidies

    Rationalise food, fertiliser and petroleum subsidies while considering their policy objectives.

    7Improve budgeting and transparency

    Use gender budgeting, outcome budgeting and performance-based budgeting to assess whether spending achieves its intended purpose.

    The central lesson is that fiscal consolidation does not simply mean cutting expenditure. It means improving the balance between revenue, spending, borrowing and development.

    0️⃣Chapter 6: Zero-Based Budgeting — every rupee must justify itself

    Imagine a department received ₹100 crore last year. This year, it asks for ₹110 crore.

    Should the government automatically approve the amount because the department received money last year? No.

    In Zero-Based Budgeting (ZBB), the department starts from zero and must justify its activities and expenditure afresh.

    The basic principle is: start from zero and justify every rupee.

    Your notes identify five features:

    1. No automatic carry-forward: Last year's allocation does not guarantee this year's funding.
    2. Bottom-up approach: Budget proposals are developed from the operational level upwards.
    3. Justification of every activity: Each unit or department must explain why its expenditure is necessary.
    4. Evaluation of alternatives: Different methods of achieving the same objective are compared.
    5. Resource optimisation: Wasteful, duplicated or unnecessary spending can be removed.

    For example, if two departments are paying for similar services, ZBB can help examine whether both expenses are necessary.

    The focus is not merely on spending less. It is on making each allocation defensible.

    🎯Chapter 7: Outcome Budgeting — what did the money actually achieve?

    Imagine the government spends ₹10,000 crore on rural roads.

    If the department reports only that it spent ₹10,000 crore, we know how much money was used, but not whether people benefited.

    An Outcome Budget connects the money allocated to measurable results.

    Your notes distinguish five components:

    OOutlay money allocated

    Example: ₹10,000 crore for rural roads.

    OOutput what was delivered

    Example: 10,000 kilometres of rural roads constructed.

    OOutcome what changed for people

    Example: Better rural connectivity and reduced travel time.

    IIndicators how progress is measured

    Example: Road length completed, travel time saved or the percentage of villages connected.

    TTimeline when results are expected

    Example: Year-wise construction targets and completion milestones.

    Your PDF gives two further examples:

    • Pradhan Mantri Gram Sadak Yojana (PMGSY): An outlay of ₹10,000 crore, an output of 10,000 km of rural roads and outcomes such as improved connectivity and reduced travel time.
    • Mid-Day Meal: An outlay of ₹12,000 crore, meals served to 10 crore students and intended outcomes such as improved nutrition and school attendance.

    Outcome budgeting improves accountability, transparency, monitoring and evaluation. It shifts attention from simply spending money to examining what the spending delivers.

    Remember the distinction: Outlay = money; output = immediate deliverable; outcome = result or impact.

    📝Chapter 8: UPSC Previous-Year Questions — test the story

    🔁Final revision: the entire chapter in one story

    The government first collects and holds money in three constitutional funds: the Consolidated Fund, Public Account and Contingency Fund. It then spends on administration, welfare and development.

    When revenue cannot cover routine expenses, a revenue deficit appears. When the government has a wider gap between total expenditure and non-borrowed receipts, it has a fiscal deficit. Subtracting interest payments gives the primary deficit, while subtracting grants for capital-asset creation from revenue deficit gives the effective revenue deficit.

    The government finances its gap through borrowing, which contributes to public debt. Excessive debt raises interest costs and can reduce the money available for development. Off-budget borrowing can hide part of the burden from the headline fiscal figures.

    The FRBM Act of 2003 attempted to impose fiscal discipline through targets, reporting and restrictions on direct RBI financing. The 2012 amendment adjusted the framework, and the N. K. Singh Committee later recommended a debt-based anchor, a fiscal-deficit glide path, an escape clause and an independent Fiscal Council.

    Finally, fiscal consolidation aims to control debt sustainably. Zero-Based Budgeting asks whether each expense deserves funding, while Outcome Budgeting asks what the money actually achieved.

    That is the complete chain: collect money → spend money → measure the deficit → borrow if necessary → manage debt → impose fiscal discipline → measure results.
