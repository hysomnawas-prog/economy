Part A

Public Finance: the basic idea

The study of how government gets money and how it spends it.

1Meaning and scope

Public income

Where does government money come from? Taxes and non-tax receipts.

Public expenditure

Where does it spend? Administration, welfare, infrastructure.

Public debt

What happens when spending is more than receipts? Borrowing, and deficits.

"Public" covers the Central Government, State Governments, local bodies (municipalities, panchayats), public corporations and statutory bodies.

How the scope has grown

Earlier it meant taxes, spending and borrowing. Now it also includes:

Social security & welfareStabilising business cyclesReducing unemploymentEquity & egalitarian societyCapital formationSustaining growth

2Musgrave's three functions

Allocation

Provide goods markets may not provide enough of, like roads and defence. Aim: efficient use of resources.

Distribution

Make income and wealth fairer through taxes and subsidies.

Stabilisation

Use fiscal tools to keep the economy steady: inflation and unemployment.

Memory aidAllocation = what gets provided. Distribution = who gets what. Stabilisation = keeping the economy steady.

Part B

Fiscal policy and the Budget

The plan, and the document that carries it out.

5Fiscal policy: meaning and objectives

Government policy on public revenue, expenditure and debt. It is formulated by the Ministry of Finance with NITI Aayog support and implemented through the annual Budget presented to Parliament.

Promote growthPrice stabilityEconomic stabilityGenerate employmentReduce inequalityControl wasteful spending

6Three types of fiscal policy

Expansionary

When: recession or slowdown.
How: spend more and/or cut taxes.
Goal: raise demand, jobs, growth.

Contractionary

When: high inflation.
How: cut spending and/or raise taxes.
Goal: control inflation, reduce deficit.

Neutral

When: economy is stable.
How: revenue ≈ expenditure.
Goal: no major push or squeeze.

7Countercyclical policy

Policies that work against the direction of the economic cycle: stimulate in a downturn, cool in a boom.

During recession or slowdown

  • Repo-rate cuts: boost credit flow
  • CRR/SLR reduced: improves liquidity
  • Capital expenditure: infra push (highways, railways)
  • Tax cuts: encourage investment (2019 corporate tax cut)
  • Welfare spending: MGNREGA, PMGKY

During boom or high inflation

  • Repo-rate hikes: curtail demand (post-COVID FY 2022)
  • Open-market sales: absorb excess liquidity
  • Government spending cuts: control overheating
  • Tax/duty hikes: reduce consumption (fuel excise)
  • Subsidy rationalisation: fiscal discipline
  • Tighter credit norms: check speculative lending

Exam clarity: repo rate and CRR/SLR are monetary instruments run by the RBI. Taxes and government spending are fiscal instruments. The source lists them together because both kinds of policy help stabilise the economy.

11Capital and revenue: receipts and expenditure

Capital expenditure (CapEx)

Creates assets or reduces liabilities.

  • Roads, railways, schools
  • Loans to States or abroad
  • Repayment of past loans

Effect: raises productive capacity.

Revenue expenditure

Creates no asset and reduces no liability. Consumptive in nature.

  • Salaries, pensions
  • Subsidies, grants
  • Interest payments
  • Administrative costs

Capital receipts

Create a liability or reduce assets. Not regular income.

  • Market borrowings
  • Disinvestment
  • Loan recoveries

Revenue receipts

Recurring, non-redeemable, no repayment liability. Used for daily functioning.

  • Taxes: GST, income tax
  • Non-tax: fines, interest, dividends
  • Grants (as listed in the source)

Some grants booked as revenue expenditure still build assets for the receiving government. That is exactly why Effective Revenue Deficit exists (Part 4).

Memory aidCapEx: asset created or liability reduced. Revenue spend: routine, no asset. Capital receipt: liability created or asset reduced. Revenue receipt: income with no repayment duty.

Part D

Deficits: meaning, formulas, interpretation

A deficit is a shortfall. Which shortfall depends on which receipts and expenses you count.

13What is a deficit?

A deficit exists when expenditure is greater than income during a period.

Deficit = Expenditure − Income (when Income < Expenditure)

The source lists six measures: budget deficit, revenue deficit, fiscal deficit, primary deficit, effective revenue deficit and monetised deficit.

14Receipts are not the same as income

Receipts

  • All money coming to government
  • Includes borrowings, income, recoveries, disinvestment
  • Used in Budget presentation

Income (for deficit sums)

  • Only non-borrowed receipts
  • Tax + non-tax revenue + non-debt capital receipts
  • Used in deficit calculations

Why leave borrowing out? It brings cash today but creates a repayment duty. Counting it as income would hide the very gap you are trying to measure.

Receipts splitItems
Income (revenue receipts)Tax, fees, fines, interest, profit, dividend
Capital receipts, non-debt creatingDisinvestment, loan recovery. No liability to repay
Borrowing (debt-creating capital receipts)Sovereign loans, loans from international financial institutions, Treasury Bills, Ways and Means Advances. Liability to repay

15–20The six deficits

Budget deficit

BD = Total expenditure − Total receipts

The source's broad shortfall measure. Modern Indian analysis tracks fiscal deficit instead.

Revenue deficit

RD = Revenue exp − Revenue receipts

Government is borrowing even for day-to-day, non-asset-creating costs. Fiscally unsustainable. Reducing it signals discipline.

Fiscal deficit

FD = Total exp − (Rev receipts + Non-debt capital receipts)

The overall borrowing requirement. Borrowings are excluded from receipts. The most tracked deficit.

Effective revenue deficit

ERD = RD − Grants for creation of capital assets

Introduced in Union Budget 2011–12. Grants to States/UTs for assets show as revenue expenditure but build assets. ERD shows the real burden of routine spending.

Primary deficit

PD = FD − Interest payments

This year's borrowing need, setting aside interest on past debt. Separates present policy from inherited burden.

Monetised deficit

MD = Net RBI credit to Government

Part of FD financed directly by the RBI (new money, or buying government securities directly). Gives flexibility in emergencies; risks inflation if overused.

Worked exampleRevenue deficit ₹5 lakh crore. Capital-asset grants ₹1 lakh crore. So ERD = 5 − 1 = ₹4 lakh crore.

Monetised deficit and the FRBM Act: routine monetisation is banned, but India keeps the option for rare, well-defined, rule-based situations, as seen during the COVID-19 crisis.

Public debt and off-budget borrowing

What the government owes, and what it keeps out of sight.

23Public debt

The total borrowing of the Central or State Government, used in part to finance fiscal deficits. It is the outstanding stock of government liabilities at a point in time.

Internal debt

Borrowed from within India: banks, financial institutions, the public.

External debt

Borrowed from foreign governments or multilaterals such as the IMF and World Bank.

Managed by the RBI under the Public Debt Act, 1944 (with modern fiscal laws updating the framework).

Higher interest burden

Interest payments eat into resources.

Government borrowing can absorb funds that private business would have used.

Rating downgrades

High debt can trigger credit-rating cuts.

Less fiscal space

Less room for future public spending.

27Off-budget borrowing

Loans and liabilities incurred to fund schemes or capital spending but not shown as ordinary government borrowing in the Budget. Raised through PSUs or special-purpose vehicles.

Part F

FRBM Act and fiscal consolidation

Rules that tried to tie the government's hands, and the road back to discipline.

28FRBM Act, 2003

BackgroundBefore 2003: high fiscal deficits, unsustainable debt, weak discipline. Post-1991 reforms showed the need for rules-based fiscal policy. The Act aims at transparency, responsibility and macro stability.
2003Original targets: Revenue deficit 0% by 2008, Fiscal deficit 3% of GDP by 2009.
2012Amendment: FD 3% by 2017, RD below 2% by 2015, ERD 0% by 2015. Medium Term Expenditure Framework (3-year rolling).
2017N.K. Singh Committee: debt-to-GDP anchor, escape clause, Fiscal Council.
2020 →Targets suspended after COVID-19. Gradual return to the path.

Provisions of the 2003 Act

  • Reduce deficits and manage public debt.
  • Government places three documents every year: Medium-Term Fiscal Policy Statement (MTFPS), Fiscal Policy Strategy Statement (FPSS), Macroeconomic Framework Statement (MFS).
  • RBI is prohibited from directly financing the fiscal deficit (no primary issuances).

Criticism

  • Targets repeatedly missed or postponed after external shocks such as the global financial crisis.
  • Too rigid in downturns.
  • Ignored quality of expenditure: was the deficit for consumption or investment?
  • Did not account for cyclical swings in the economy.

29FRBM Amendment, 2012

Allowed postponement of the 3% goal and added more realistic timeframes. It introduced Effective Revenue Deficit and the Medium Term Expenditure Framework.

Part G

Public revenue and taxation

Where the money comes from, and why taxes are shaped the way they are.

35Public revenue

Income received from all sources for public expenditure, excluding borrowings.

Tax revenue

Compulsory payments with no direct quid pro quo.

Income Tax, GST, Customs Duty.

Non-tax revenue

Income from sources other than taxes.

Fees, fines, PSU dividends, interest on loans to States.

36–37Taxation: meaning, features, objectives

A compulsory contribution imposed by lawful authority to fund public spending. The taxpayer gets no direct, equal service for each payment. Benefits arrive indirectly as public services. It is the biggest revenue source for modern governments and a key tool of fiscal policy.

Features

  • Compulsory for eligible taxpayers
  • Imposed by lawful authority: Parliament or a State Legislature
  • No direct return (non-quid pro quo)
  • Primary income stream

Economic objectives

  • Mobilise resources for public expenditure
  • Reduce income and wealth inequality
  • Control inflation / manage aggregate demand

Governance objectives

  • Strengthen the state's fiscal capacity
  • Nudge behaviour: sin tax, green tax
  • Promote investment in priority sectors

42Black money

Unaccounted income or wealth not reported to tax authorities. Not taxed, not disclosed.

Sources

Legal but hidden: under-invoicing, real-estate undervaluation, unreported business income.

Illegal: bribery, smuggling, hawala, drug trade, betting, extortion.

Impacts

  • Loss of tax revenue
  • Distorted income distribution and data
  • Promotes corruption and a parallel economy
  • Weakens monetary and fiscal policy

Steps to curb it

Law and enforcement

  • Black Money (Undisclosed Foreign Income and Assets) Act, 2015
  • Benami Transactions (Prohibition) Amendment Act, 2016
  • Stronger PMLA provisions

Policy

  • Demonetisation (2016)
  • PAN–Aadhaar linking
  • Income Declaration Schemes

Administration and tech

  • Digital payments push
  • Operation Clean Money
  • Tighter reporting of high-value transactions

43Trade remedies: anti-dumping and countervailing duty

Anti-dumping duty

  • Protects domestic industry from dumped imports (sold in India below normal value in the exporter's market)
  • Levied on top of normal customs duty
  • After investigation by DGTR (Ministry of Commerce)
  • Based on WTO Agreement on Anti-Dumping (GATT 1994)
  • Duty = Normal Value − Export Price to India
  • Valid generally for 5 years, extendable after review
  • Section 9A, Customs Tariff Act, 1975
  • Example: Chinese steel, solar cells, certain chemicals

Countervailing duty (CVD)

  • Offsets subsidies given by exporting countries
  • Under the Customs Tariff Act, 1975, in line with WTO Agreement on Subsidies and Countervailing Measures
  • DGTR recommends; Government of India imposes
  • Neutralises the price advantage of subsidised imports
  • Example: steel from a country subsidising its makers
  • With GST, CVD was subsumed into IGST on imports

44Transfer pricing and APA

Transfer pricing

Pricing of goods, services or intangibles moved between related entities of a multinational in different tax jurisdictions. Risk: shifting profits to low-tax countries (BEPS, Base Erosion and Profit Shifting).

India: Sections 92 to 92F, Income Tax Act, 1961, on the Arm's Length Principle: price as if between unrelated parties.

Example: an Indian subsidiary sells software to its foreign parent at an artificially low price.

Advance Pricing Agreement (APA)

A pre-agreed transfer-pricing method between taxpayer and tax authority for a fixed period. Brings certainty, avoids disputes.

  • Unilateral: taxpayer + CBDT
  • Bilateral: taxpayer, CBDT, another country's authority (tax treaty)
  • Multilateral: several countries

Benefits: less litigation, certainty, trust. Introduced by Finance Act 2012, Section 92CC.

45Laffer curve

Shows the link between tax rates and tax revenue. Past a point, higher rates cut total revenue because they discourage work, investment and compliance, and encourage evasion.

Key idea

At 0% tax, revenue = 0. At 100% tax, revenue = 0. Maximum revenue sits at an optimal rate between the two. Popularised by Arthur Laffer in the 1970s.

Policy and criticism

Avoid excessively high rates that shrink the base. Example: India's 2019 corporate tax cut. Criticism: the optimal rate isn't fixed; it varies by economy, compliance culture and time.

46Fiscal drag

Inflation-driven income growth pushes taxpayers into higher brackets (bracket creep), raising their tax burden even if real income hasn't improved.

ChainHigh inflation → higher nominal income → higher tax burden → lower spending → economic slowdown (fiscal drag).
  • Impact: acts as an automatic stabiliser by restraining demand in inflation, but can slow recovery if it cuts demand too much.
  • Remedy: index tax brackets to inflation; adjust allowances and deductions.

47Tax-to-GDP ratio

Share of total tax revenue (direct + indirect) in GDP. It measures tax mobilisation capacity and fiscal strength. A higher ratio gives more fiscal space for welfare and infrastructure; a low ratio means more borrowing and less policy room.

India (2023–24 est.)

Centre + States: about 11–12%
Centre alone: about 6–7%
OECD average: about 34%

Why it is low in India

  • Large informal sector
  • Low tax compliance
  • Agricultural income largely untaxed
  • Multiple exemptions and incentives

48–49Tax buoyancy and tax elasticity

Tax buoyancy

Responsiveness of tax revenue growth to GDP growth.

  • > 1: revenue grows faster than GDP (good: rising compliance or rates)
  • = 1: same pace
  • < 1: slower than GDP (weak buoyancy)

Shows tax-system efficiency and helps forecast revenue. A tax is buoyant when it collects more without any change in rate.

Tax elasticity

How much tax revenue or the tax base changes when the tax rate changes. It gauges how sensitive taxpayers are.

High elasticity: a small rate change causes a large change in the base. Low elasticity: the base barely responds.

Part H

Goods and Services Tax (GST)

"One Nation, One Tax, One Market."

50Core idea

A comprehensive indirect tax on the supply of goods and services. It merged most indirect taxes of the Union and States, charges tax on value addition at each stage, and reduces tax-on-tax (cascading).

101stConstitutional Amendment Act, 2016
Art. 279AGST Council
1 Jul 2017Effective date
Dual GSTCGST + SGST intra-State, IGST inter-State

Replaced: excise duty, VAT, service tax, octroi, entry tax and others.

Input Tax Credit (ITC)

Credit a business claims for GST paid on its purchases, to reduce GST on its sales. Purpose: avoid cascading, so tax falls only on the value added at each stage.

ExampleA manufacturer pays ₹300 GST on raw materials and collects ₹450 GST on the final product. After claiming ₹300 as ITC, it pays the government only ₹150.

51Administration: the four GSTs

CGST

Central GST, levied by the Centre.

SGST

State GST, levied by States.

UTGST

For Union Territories without a legislature.

IGST

Inter-State supplies and imports. Apportioned between the Centre and the destination State.

CGST and SGST are levied together on a common base across the value chain, a dual model that suits India's federal structure.

52Destination-based tax

Revenue accrues to the State where the goods or services are consumed, not where they are produced.

Old system (origin-based)GST (destination-based)
Producing State gets revenueConsuming State gets revenue
Excise Duty, Central Sales Tax (CST)IGST, shared with the consuming State
ExampleA product made in Gujarat is sold to a customer in Kerala. Kerala, the consuming State, gets the tax through IGST settlement. Gujarat gets nothing from that transaction.

53GST Council

Constitutional body (101st Amendment, 2016) that recommends on rates, exemptions, thresholds and model laws.

Composition

  • Union Finance Minister: Chairperson
  • Union Minister of State (Finance): Member
  • Finance/Taxation Minister of each State: Members

Voting

  • Centre: 1/3 weight (33%)
  • States combined: 2/3 weight (66%)
  • Decisions need a 3/4 majority (75%)

Functions

  • Recommend rates, exemptions, thresholds
  • Decide GST laws, rules, procedures
  • Resolve inter-State issues and disputes
  • Special provisions for North-Eastern and hill States
  • Compensation to States (first 5 years)
  • Rate rationalisation, fixing inverted duty

Significance

  • Cooperative federalism
  • Consensus-based tax governance
  • Uniform indirect taxation
  • Delivers "One Nation, One Tax, One Market"
  • Eases compliance

54Applicability and thresholds

  • Applies to all goods and services except alcohol for human consumption.
  • Five petroleum products (crude oil, petrol, diesel, natural gas, ATF): GST from a date the Council will notify.
  • Exempt: certain essentials such as education, healthcare, milk, fresh fruits.
  • Exports are zero-rated: no GST, and ITC is refundable.

  • Cesses on petroleum, tobacco, coal

  • Purchase Tax

56Rate slabs after GST 2.0

5%

Household essentials (soaps, toothpaste, Indian breads), life-saving drugs (down from 12%), farm machinery and irrigation equipment (down from 12%). Some at Nil.

18%

Middle-class items: two-wheelers, small cars, TVs, ACs, cement (down from 28%).

40%

Sin and luxury goods: tobacco, pan masala, aerated drinks, luxury goods.

Structure: two slabs, 5% and 18%, plus the 40% rate. Petroleum is in GST constitutionally but not yet levied. Alcohol is outside GST by constitutional design.

Revenue Neutral Rate (RNR)

The single GST rate at which total tax revenue stays the same as before GST. It ensures no revenue loss for Centre and States in the transition. Calculated from pre-GST revenue (Excise, Service Tax, VAT and others), adjusted for credit. India's RNR was recommended at 15–15.5% by the Subramanian Committee (2015). It guided the earlier 5/12/18/28 slabs.

57GSTN, returns and GSTIN

GST Network (GSTN)

Not-for-profit Section 8 company under the Companies Act, 2013, giving IT infrastructure to Centre, States and taxpayers. Initially 51% private and 49% government; since 2018, 100% government-owned (shared equally by Centre and States).

Provides the common portal: registration, returns, payments, refunds, invoice and ITC matching. Integrates with CBDT, Customs, banks. Ensures uniform administration and curbs evasion through invoice matching.

58–60Reverse charge, inverted duty, e-way bill

Reverse Charge Mechanism

The liability to pay GST shifts from the supplier to the recipient.

  • Specified notified goods/services
  • Import of services
  • Purchases from unregistered dealers in certain cases

Recipient pays in cash (ITC can't pay it) but can later claim ITC on it if used for business. Aim: curb evasion, bring unorganised sectors into the net.

Inverted duty structure

GST on inputs is higher than GST on the finished product.

Result: unused ITC piles up and working capital gets blocked.

Example: raw material 18%, finished product 12%. Remedy: refund of unutilised ITC under Section 54(3) CGST Act (not for certain goods such as textiles in notified cases).

E-way bill

Electronic document for moving goods valued above ₹50,000, generated on the GSTN portal, SMS, mobile app or API. Unique E-Way Bill Number (EBN) goes to supplier, recipient and transporter.

Validity: 1 day up to 200 km, +1 day per additional 200 km. Over-dimensional cargo: 1 day per 20 km.

E-way bill: who generates

Supplier or recipient if moving goods themselves. Transporter if neither has done so.

E-way bill: exemptions

Goods ≤ ₹50,000; non-motorised vehicles; specified goods (fresh fruit, vegetables, milk); special cases such as customs-bonded movement.

Objectives and issues

Prevent evasion, track movement in real time, smooth logistics. Challenges: technical glitches, compliance delays. Linking to FASTag and AI fraud detection could help.

62QRMP scheme

Quarterly Return, Monthly Payment, for taxpayers with turnover up to ₹5 crore. Optional.

How it works

  • GSTR-3B filed quarterly
  • Tax paid monthly: fixed-sum method (pre-filled challan, about 35% of last quarter's tax) or self-assessment on actual liability
  • B2B invoices uploaded monthly for the first 2 months of a quarter
  • Buyers claim ITC without waiting for quarter-end

Benefits

  • 4 returns instead of 12
  • Can make inter-State supplies
  • Flexible payment method
  • Can issue tax invoices and collect GST
  • Continuous ITC flow for buyers; good for B2B

63E-invoicing

Electronic authentication of B2B invoices by GSTN through the Invoice Registration Portal (IRP). Mandatory for businesses with turnover above ₹10 crore from 1 October 2022.

1Supplier uploads the B2B invoice to the IRP
2IRP issues an Invoice Reference Number (IRN), a digitally signed e-invoice and a QR code
3Buyer receives the invoice with QR code and IRN
4Data auto-flows to the GST Portal and E-Way Bill Portal

Purpose: standard invoice formats, system-to-system readability, auto-populated returns, fewer reconciliation problems, real-time reporting against evasion.

Exempt: SEZ units (developers may be covered); banks, NBFCs, insurers; goods transport agencies (road); passenger transport; admission to multiplex cinemas.

64GST Compensation Cess

  • Purpose: compensate States/UTs for GST revenue loss over a 5-year transition (2017–2022).
  • Basis: assumed 14% annual growth in State GST revenue over base year 2015–16.
  • Levied on luxury and sin goods on top of GST: tobacco products, aerated drinks, motor vehicles, coal, lignite, peat.
  • Collected by the Centre into the GST Compensation Fund, used only to compensate States.
  • Shortfall: collections fell short, especially in the 2020 COVID lockdown, so the Centre borrowed.
  • Extension: the levy continued beyond 30 June 2022 to repay those pandemic-era compensation loans. The Centre's statutory obligation ended; no fresh compensation is paid.
  • Law: GST (Compensation to States) Act, 2017. Administered by the Department of Revenue.

65Kerala Flood Cess

When and why

From 1 June 2019 for 2 years (till May 2021), to fund Rebuild Kerala after the 2018 floods.

Rate

Extra 1% on goods and services with GST above 5%.

Scope

Not on railway services, movie tickets, or goods/services at 5% or nil. Only intra-State transactions within Kerala.

66Advance ruling

A written decision by tax authorities on GST questions before a transaction. It gives clarity, cuts disputes and improves compliance.

  • Authority for Advance Ruling (AAR) in each State/UT; appeals go to the AAAR.
  • Covers: classification, applicability of a notification, time and value of supply, ITC eligibility, determination of liability.
  • Binding on the applicant and the jurisdictional GST officer while facts stay the same.

67NAA and GSTAT

National Anti-Profiteering Authority (NAA)

  • Statutory body under CGST Act, 2017
  • Ensures GST rate cuts reach consumers as lower prices
  • Can impose penalties, order refunds (unidentifiable consumers: Consumer Welfare Fund), act on complaints or suo motu
  • Initially 2 years, extended twice; last term ended 30 Nov 2022
  • Job shifted to the Competition Commission of India

GST Appellate Tribunal (GSTAT)

  • Statutory body under CGST Act, 2017
  • Hears appeals against first appellate authorities
  • One national forum for Centre–State GST disputes; lightens High Court load
  • Principal Bench: New Delhi; about 31 State/Regional Benches across about 45 locations
  • Each bench: a Judicial Member plus technical members from Centre and State
  • President: Justice (Retd.) Sanjaya Kumar Mishra
  • Now the national appellate authority for advance-ruling disputes
  • Digital: e-Courts portal, virtual hearings
  • Legacy appeals: file by 30 June 2026 (orders till 31 Mar 2026). Normal limit: 3 months (orders after 1 Apr 2026)
  • Backlog about 4 lakh appeals; 116 members, 32 benches

Key issues: transition and backlog; balance of Centre–State technical members; timely judgments.

68Cascading and the common national market

How GST removed cascading

  • One system: CGST, SGST/UTGST, IGST
  • Seamless ITC from manufacturing to wholesale to retail
  • Tax only on value addition
  • Fewer hidden costs

How it built a common market

  • Uniform tax structure across States
  • IGST smooths inter-State trade, no cascading like old CST
  • E-way bill removes check-post delays
  • Easier business, more competitive industry

Current updates (2025 context)

1.51 crore

GST taxpayers in 2025, up from 66.5 lakh in 2017.

₹2.04 lakh crore

Average monthly collection, up from ₹82,000 crore in 2017–18.

~30%

GST's share of the Centre's gross tax revenue (Budget 2025–26).

States were compensated for revenue loss till June 2022; the post-compensation period shows higher SGST buoyancy. GST also helped India climb the World Bank Ease of Doing Business ranking earlier.

Mains-ready closeGST (2017) broadened the base, improved buoyancy and created a unified market. The next phase, GST 2.0, aims at a simplified two-rate structure, seamless digital compliance (e-invoicing, e-way bills) and dispute resolution through the newly operational GSTAT (2025), supporting formalisation and competitiveness for a developed India by 2047.
